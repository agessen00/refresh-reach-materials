Executive summary, the business case in one slide
Own the users. Do not rent the audience.
Problem
Access is fragmented, and value sits inside each partner's door. When a partner leaves, refuses to pay, or falls out of favor, one of our most important models turns volatile, and the company is worth less.
Insight
Give each person a portable REACH account that sits above any single org. The entry door becomes an on-ramp rather than a gate, so we keep the user even when the partner changes.
Model
Acquire through trusted, marquee providers. Then activate, retain, monetize, and grow referrals for every user on one shared REACH network. That funnel is AARRR.
Market
Roughly 100 million Americans live below the true cost of living. We enter through Goodwill, the YMCA, Catholic Charities, and their peers, with millions of licenses already within reach.
Outcome
A durable, defensible asset, which is millions of active users we own and keep, measured by the user-side and business-side KPIs set out in this plan.
The framework this plan is built on
Two proven frameworks: Pirate Metrics and Aggregation Theory.
The funnel is AARRR, the Pirate Metrics, which are Acquisition, Activation, Retention, Revenue, and Referral, coined by Dave McClure (500 Startups, 2007).
Platforms that own the customer relationship directly, rather than reaching people only through distributors or partners, earn compounding network effects. As more value flows through the platform, users stay, and customer-acquisition cost falls once you pass critical mass.
Aggregation Theory, Ben Thompson, Stratechery (2015). Network-effects theory: a technology's value grows with its number of users.

American Express and card networks

Co-branded cards such as the Delta SkyMiles Card ride on the Amex network. The membership, rewards, and data belong to the network, and they are portable across every brand on it.

Shopify and Shop Pay

Every merchant owns its storefront, yet Shop Pay is one portable identity that travels with the shopper across every Shopify brand. Hundreds of millions of buyers are recognized network-wide.

REACH applies the same pattern to human services. Many trusted, branded front doors rest on one portable network and one portable account.
Our North Star, the real goal, not today's task
Millions of individuals, reached through many trusted doors, onboarded into one platform.
Providers people already trust bring them in and onboard them. Technology carries them, but the point is what those millions unlock once they are ours to serve, which is economies of scale for curated access, driving wellbeing outward, ring by ring.
Winning a local brand's app is today's task, and it matters. Yet it is only acquisition and activation. It is not the goal, and we should not mistake the task for the goal.

Our business model, the reminder that anchors everything
Grow, and never lose, the number of active users in our environment.
AARRR, the Pirate Metrics, which are Acquisition, Activation, Retention, Revenue, and Referral, the five behavioral metrics of a growth business, coined by Dave McClure (500 Startups, 2007).
A
Acquisition

We ride in on marquee, trusted providers such as Goodwill, the YMCA, and Catholic Charities. We are introduced to the audience, and we do not buy it.

A
Activation

Engagement through the org's existing relationship and programs, and through our rewards and perks.

R
Retention

They come back, again and again. Because the account is portable, they do not fall off when a program ends.

R
Revenue

They buy through the marketplace, and businesses pay us for access to a trusted, engaged audience.

R
Referral

Other businesses want in because of our reach, and users invite others into access they value.

Portability protects every stage. Keep the active user, and acquisition compounds into retention, revenue, and referral rather than leaking out the door.
Framework: Dave McClure, Startup Metrics for Pirates (500 Startups, 2007).

Sustainability and defensibility, the risk we design out
Partners are channels, not the switch on our value.
If our leverage lives inside any single nonprofit or connector, variables we do not control become our fate. One leaves. One will not pay. People no longer want to work with that org. That turns our model volatile, then obsolete, and makes the company worth less.
If value lives in the partner

Partner churn, price resistance, or a reputational falling-out with one org can wipe out the audience, and the revenue with it. Our worth rises and falls on decisions made outside our walls.

If value lives in the person and the network

Any single door can open or close and our active users stay, because the account and the REACH network are ours. Partner-dependent leverage becomes a durable, owned asset.

The rule: orgs are how people come in. They are not allowed to be how our value walks out.

Why the model changed, and the naming with it
Goodwill: from Refresh@Goodwill to Goodwill Marion, Ohio, powered by REACH.
This was not because Goodwills did not want their own app. The real reason is business model and velocity of adoption. A federated national rollout would never move at the pace we need. Refresh@Goodwill was one operational reason, not the strategic driver.

The Amex analogy: the brand rides on the network, not the other way around

A Delta SkyMiles Card is co-branded, but it rides on the Amex network. Amex does not ride on Delta. Amex Platinum is Amex direct. Goodwill Marion, CCDSD, and the YMCA are the co-branded cards. The neutral REACH app is like Platinum. Everything rides on REACH, and REACH rides on none of them.

The opportunity, why now
We have a rare, one-time chance to own our leverage, and to let it scale as we grow.
This is fundamentally different from our current enterprise relationships. There, value equals the contract. Here, value equals the millions we onboard and keep. We do not build an opportunity to lose our leverage. We build the one that compounds it.
